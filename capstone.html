13  A monthly situation report

Up to now every chapter has handed you a question with the shape of the answer already showing. Filter this, group by that, plot the result. Real analysis doesn’t arrive in that shape. It arrives as a request from someone who wants to know what happened, by Friday, in a form they can put in front of a committee.

So here is the request. You pick a month of Singapore’s 2020 and write the report. I’ll do April first so you can see where the bar is, then August is yours.

13.1 The brief

One month. One page. On that page:

a table that gives the reader the month in numbers, two figures that show something the table can’t, and three sentences of interpretation. Three sentences, not three paragraphs. The sentence limit is the hard part and it’s deliberate. Anyone can produce eight paragraphs of hedged description. Deciding which three things matter is the work.

Assume the reader is a public health officer who knows Singapore and does not know R. They will not read your code. They will look at the figures first, the table second, and your sentences only if the figures made them curious.

13.2 What a good report contains

There are six things I look for, and they are all things you already know how to do.

  1. The month is stated, and so is the denominator. How many days does the month have, and how many of them carry data? Every month in this file is complete, but saying so is what separates a report from an assumption.
  2. A count and a shape. A total tells you the size of the month. A median, a maximum and the date of the maximum tell you whether that total came from a steady grind or one bad day. Report both.
  3. A table with a unit the reader can hold. Days are too fine for a monthly table and the month itself is too coarse. Weeks work.
  4. Two figures that answer different questions. Two views of the case curve is one figure drawn twice. Pair the epidemic curve with something else in the file: mobility, the stringency index, the severity mix.
  5. Every figure has a title that states a finding. “Daily cases, April 2020” is a label. “Cases rose for 13 days after the circuit breaker began” is a finding. Titles that make a claim force you to check the claim.
  6. At least one caveat that comes from looking at the data, not from a template. “Data may be incomplete” is a template. “The 5 August count is four times the next highest August day and needs an explanation before it goes in a mean” is analysis.

13.3 April 2020, worked

13.3.1 A script that stands on its own

Your report script must be readable from the top without any hidden state. That means it reads the file and builds its own columns, in view, before it uses them. This is exactly the pipeline from the conditionals chapter, written out once so nothing in the report depends on something you can’t see.

covid <- read_covid() %>%
  arrange(date) %>%
  mutate(
    daily_cases  = confirmed - lag(confirmed, default = 0),
    daily_deaths = deaths - lag(deaths, default = 0),
    severity = case_when(
      daily_cases <  100 ~ "Low",
      daily_cases <= 300 ~ "Medium",
      daily_cases >  300 ~ "High"
    ),
    severity = factor(severity, levels = c("Low", "Medium", "High")),
    month = floor_date(date, "month")
  )

If you’re writing this in a script of your own rather than inside the book’s project, swap read_covid() for read_csv(here("data", "sg_covid_data.csv")). That helper exists so the chapters don’t repeat a file path forty times, and nothing else in the pipeline depends on it.

Then pick the month once, at the top, in a named object. When August comes round you change one line rather than hunting for "2020-04" scattered through twenty chunks.

report_month <- as.Date("2020-04-01")

april <- covid %>%
  filter(month == report_month)

nrow(april)
#> [1] 30

Thirty rows for a thirty-day month. That check takes two seconds and it is the cheapest error you will ever catch.

13.3.2 How heavy was the month

ImportantPredict before you run this

April 2020 has one day that dwarfs the rest. Write down the date you think it fell on before you read the next output. Most people put it in the first week, on the theory that the circuit breaker started on 7 April and things improved from there.

april %>%
  summarise(
    days         = n(),
    cases        = sum(daily_cases),
    mean_daily   = mean(daily_cases),
    median_daily = median(daily_cases),
    peak         = max(daily_cases),
    peak_date    = date[which.max(daily_cases)],
    deaths       = sum(daily_deaths)
  )
#> # A tibble: 1 × 7
#>    days cases mean_daily median_daily  peak peak_date  deaths
#>   <int> <dbl>      <dbl>        <dbl> <dbl> <date>      <dbl>
#> 1    30 15243       508.         488.  1426 2020-04-20     12

The peak is 20 April, thirteen days after the circuit breaker began. Cases went up, and kept going up, for two weeks after Singapore closed workplaces and told everyone to stay home.

That is not a paradox and it is worth being precise about why. Reported cases are the output of a testing programme, not a direct measurement of infection. Through April, testing in the migrant-worker dormitories was expanding fast, and the dormitory outbreak dominated the national count from April to August. A rising reported curve during that period is partly transmission and partly the swab rate catching up with infections that had already happened. You cannot separate the two from this file, which is a thing to say in your report rather than a thing to hide.

Two small notes on that output. median_daily prints as 488. with a trailing full stop, which is the tibble telling you the value isn’t a whole number. It’s 487.5, the average of the two middle days. And deaths is 12 here, which is trustworthy for April because the deaths column has no missing values from 21 March onwards. Earlier in the year it does, 59 leading NAs, and lag-differencing across them recovers only 27 of the year’s 29 deaths. If your chosen month is January, February or March, say that out loud.

april %>%
  count(severity)
#> # A tibble: 3 × 2
#>   severity     n
#>   <fct>    <int>
#> 1 Low          5
#> 2 Medium       7
#> 3 High        18

Eighteen of April’s thirty days were over 300 cases. Across the whole year only 77 days were, so April holds nearly a quarter of the year’s worst days in a single month.

13.3.3 The table

Weeks are the right grain for a monthly table. floor_date(date, "week", week_start = 1) puts each date on its Monday. I set week_start explicitly because lubridate’s default is Sunday and half the people reading this expect Monday. Making the assumption visible costs one argument.

april_weeks <- april %>%
  mutate(week = floor_date(date, "week", week_start = 1)) %>%
  group_by(week) %>%
  summarise(
    days       = n(),
    cases      = sum(daily_cases),
    mean_daily = round(mean(daily_cases)),
    peak       = max(daily_cases),
    retail     = round(mean(retail_and_recreation_percent_change_from_baseline)),
    stringency = round(mean(stringency_index), 1)
  )

april_weeks
#> # A tibble: 5 × 7
#>   week        days cases mean_daily  peak retail stringency
#>   <date>     <int> <dbl>      <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl>      <dbl>
#> 1 2020-03-30     5   383         77   120    -23       41.7
#> 2 2020-04-06     7  1223        175   287    -53       71  
#> 3 2020-04-13     7  4056        579   942    -61       76.8
#> 4 2020-04-20     7  7036       1005  1426    -67       77.6
#> 5 2020-04-27     4  2545        636   799    -67       76.8

Look at the days column before you look at anything else. The first row covers five days, not seven, because April 2020 started on a Wednesday and the week beginning 30 March mostly belongs to March. The last row covers four. Any comparison of cases across those rows is comparing different amounts of time, which is why mean_daily is in the table and why days is in it too. Leave days out and a reader will read 383 against 1,223 as a threefold rise when part of it is nothing more than two extra days.

The rest of the table tells a fairly clean story. Weekly totals climb from 383 to 7,036 and then fall back. Mean retail mobility drops from 23% below baseline in the first partial week to 67% below in each of the last two. Mean stringency goes from 41.7 to 71 in one step and sits in the seventies for the rest of the month.

13.3.4 The first figure: the epidemic curve

cb_start <- as.Date("2020-04-07")

ggplot(april, aes(date, daily_cases)) +
  geom_col(fill = nus_colours[["blue"]]) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = cb_start, colour = nus_colours[["crimson"]],
             linetype = "dashed") +
  annotate("text", x = cb_start + 1, y = 1350, hjust = 0, size = 3.2,
           colour = nus_colours[["crimson"]], label = "Circuit breaker begins") +
  scale_x_date(date_breaks = "1 week", date_labels = "%d %b") +
  scale_y_continuous(labels = label_comma()) +
  labs(
    title = "Cases rose for 13 days after the circuit breaker began",
    subtitle = "Singapore, new COVID-19 cases reported each day, April 2020",
    x = NULL, y = "New cases reported",
    caption = "Source: COVID-19 Data Hub. Reported cases, not infections."
  )

Columns rather than a line, because these are counts of things that happened on discrete days and a line implies you could read a value off it halfway between two dates. The dashed rule and the label do the work that a paragraph of text would otherwise have to do. The caption carries the source and the one warning the reader most needs.

The date breaks are weekly and the labels are "%d %b", so the axis reads 06 Apr, 13 Apr, 20 Apr. Left to itself ggplot2 would have chosen something reasonable but not necessarily aligned with the weeks in your table, and a reader who is trying to match figure to table will notice.

13.3.5 The second figure: what people actually did

The case curve says what was reported. The mobility columns say what people did, which is a different question and the reason this file is more interesting than a case line on its own.

Two series on one set of axes means one row per series per date, so the two columns have to become one column of values plus one column of labels. pivot_longer() does that. It’s from tidyr, which came in with the tidyverse, and it’s the only function in this chapter you haven’t already met.

april_mobility <- april %>%
  select(
    date,
    `Retail and recreation` = retail_and_recreation_percent_change_from_baseline,
    Workplaces              = workplaces_percent_change_from_baseline
  ) %>%
  pivot_longer(-date, names_to = "series", values_to = "change")

april_mobility
#> # A tibble: 60 × 3
#>    date       series                change
#>    <date>     <chr>                  <dbl>
#>  1 2020-04-01 Retail and recreation    -23
#>  2 2020-04-01 Workplaces               -22
#>  3 2020-04-02 Retail and recreation    -25
#>  4 2020-04-02 Workplaces               -27
#>  5 2020-04-03 Retail and recreation    -21
#>  6 2020-04-03 Workplaces               -20
#>  7 2020-04-04 Retail and recreation    -24
#>  8 2020-04-04 Workplaces                -7
#>  9 2020-04-05 Retail and recreation    -23
#> 10 2020-04-05 Workplaces               -10
#> # ℹ 50 more rows

Renaming inside select() is what gets you a readable legend without a second call. Backticks around Retail and recreation because it has spaces in it; R will accept a name like that as long as you quote it that way, and here it’s worth it because that string goes straight onto the plot.

ggplot(april_mobility, aes(date, change, colour = series)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = nus_colours[["muted"]], linewidth = 0.3) +
  geom_line() +
  geom_vline(xintercept = cb_start, colour = nus_colours[["crimson"]],
             linetype = "dashed") +
  scale_x_date(date_breaks = "1 week", date_labels = "%d %b") +
  labs(
    title = "Movement fell the day the rules changed, and stayed down",
    subtitle = "Percent change from Google's 3 January to 6 February 2020 baseline",
    x = NULL, y = "Change from baseline (%)", colour = NULL,
    caption = "Weekends are not comparable with weekdays: the baseline is a median by day of week."
  )

The step at 7 April is the whole point of the figure. Retail goes from 15% below baseline on 6 April to 51% below on 7 April; workplaces go from 24% below to 52% below on the same day. Behaviour changed overnight. The case curve took a fortnight to turn.

Now the two things in that figure that are not what they look like. The deep spike down to 83% below baseline is 10 April, which was Good Friday, a public holiday. The eight deepest workplace-mobility days in the entire year are all public holidays, so any dip you see in this series should be checked against a calendar before it’s called a policy effect.

The regular bumps upwards are weekends, and they run the opposite way to intuition. Google’s baseline is a median computed separately for each day of the week, so a Sunday is compared with pre-pandemic Sundays. Workplace activity on a normal Sunday was already low, which leaves less room to fall.

april %>%
  filter(date >= cb_start) %>%
  mutate(weekday = wday(date, label = TRUE)) %>%
  group_by(weekday) %>%
  summarise(
    days       = n(),
    workplaces = mean(workplaces_percent_change_from_baseline)
  )
#> # A tibble: 7 × 3
#>   weekday  days workplaces
#>   <ord>   <int>      <dbl>
#> 1 Sun         3      -50  
#> 2 Mon         3      -66  
#> 3 Tue         4      -63  
#> 4 Wed         4      -66.5
#> 5 Thu         4      -67  
#> 6 Fri         3      -71.7
#> 7 Sat         3      -52.3

Weekdays sit between 63 and 72 points below baseline. Saturday and Sunday sit around 50. Nobody went back to the office at the weekend. The denominator changed.

If you want the two figures stacked as a single image for a one-page PDF, patchwork will do it with p_cases / p_mobility once you’ve assigned each plot to a name. I’ve kept them separate here so each keeps its full height on screen.

13.3.6 The three sentences

April carried 15,243 reported cases, 26% of Singapore’s 2020 total, with 18 of its 30 days above 300 cases and 12 deaths; only May, at 18,715, was heavier.

Reported cases kept climbing for 13 days after the circuit breaker took effect on 7 April, peaking at 1,426 on 20 April, a pattern that reflects the pace of testing in the migrant-worker dormitories as much as the timing of infection.

Movement changed the day the rules did, with retail activity falling from 15% below baseline on 6 April to 51% below on 7 April and staying within a few points of its floor for the rest of the month, so the behavioural response was immediate even though the case curve was not.

Three sentences, three different jobs. The first sizes the month against the year. The second addresses the thing a reader will get wrong if you don’t. The third brings in the second data source and draws the contrast that makes the report worth reading.

Notice what isn’t in there. No recommendation, because nothing in this file supports one. No claim that the circuit breaker worked or didn’t, because a single month of a single country with no counterfactual cannot tell you. A situation report describes the situation. Resist the pull to conclude.

13.4 What this analysis does not tell you

This section is not decoration. Write a version of it for your own month, and make it specific.

Raw counts ignore population. Fifteen thousand cases is a number of people, not a rate. To turn it into a rate you need a denominator, and the honest denominator here is not obvious. Roughly 323,000 work-permit holders lived in dormitories of all types in 2020, of whom about 200,000 were in the 43 purpose-built dormitories. A case rate computed over the national population and a case rate computed over the dormitory-resident population are different quantities, and in April 2020 they were not remotely close. This file gives you neither denominator.

Raw counts ignore testing volume. A reported case requires a test, a positive result and a report. Change the number of tests and the case count changes without a single extra infection. There’s no test-count column in this file, so you cannot compute test positivity, which is the standard way of asking whether a rise in cases is a rise in transmission or a rise in looking. When your curve rises, you are entitled to say that reported cases rose. You are not entitled to say that infections rose.

One national count is averaging two epidemics. From April to August 2020 the dormitory outbreak dominated Singapore’s case counts, and it had different dynamics from transmission in the wider community: different living density, different age structure, different testing regime, different intervention. MOH’s own daily updates reported those streams separately at the time. This file carries a single national total, so every mean you compute over those months is a weighted average of two processes, with weights you can’t see and that change from week to week. That is a limitation of the file, not of the epidemiology, and you should say which one you’re reporting.

5 August 2020 is the sharpest example. That day records 908 cases, against an August median of 91 and a next-highest August day of 313. Nine hundred and three of those 908 were dormitory residents, and 581 were linked to a single cluster at Acacia Lodge. MOH attributed the day’s numbers to clearance testing in dormitories picking up past infections. Put that day in a mean without comment and you have published a wave that did not happen on that day.

One more, and I’m flagging it as my own reading rather than something you can verify from the data. Google’s mobility series are built from location history on the phones of users who have that setting switched on, and Google does not publish these series broken down by nationality or work-permit status. Whether the movement of people confined to dormitories under quarantine appears in these curves at all is not a question this file can answer. I would not lean on mobility to describe what happened inside the dormitories in April. That is the difference between running the code and doing the epidemiology: the code will compute a mean of anything you hand it.

13.5 Your turn

Exercise 13.1 (Write the August 2020 situation report) Produce the one-page report for August 2020: a table, two figures, three sentences of interpretation, and a short note on what your analysis does not tell you.

August is a harder month than April, and not because the code is harder. The code is the same code. August is harder because the month contains a decision that April doesn’t, and how you handle it is most of what separates a good report from a competent one.

You are done when someone who has never seen the data could read your page and come away with a correct impression of what August 2020 was like.

NoteStuck? Open for a hint (not the answer)

Compute the maximum, the median and the date of the maximum before you compute anything else, and put those three numbers next to each other. If a single day is several times the next highest day, the mean is describing that day rather than the month, and your report has to decide out loud what to do about it.

Worked solution

If you want a second month after that, September 2020 is the quiet counterpart: 30 days, every one of them under 100 cases, a mean of 31.8. Writing an interesting report about a month where nothing happens is its own skill, and you will be asked for one eventually.
